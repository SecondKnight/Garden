Before we divine the causality between Charlies Babbage’s work and Adam Smith's principles, we must first write these principles in his own words. 

Adam Smith said that "The great increase in the quantity of work, which, in consequence of the division of labour, the same number of people are capable of performing, is owing to three different circumstances: 
- First, to the increase of dexterity in every particular workman; 
- Secondly, to the saving of time, which is commonly lost in passing from one species of work to another; 
- Lastly, to the invention of a great number of machines which facilitate and abridge labour and enable one do the work of many." 

While Babbage's argument did draw from these words, I find that he took Smith's idea on saving time and expanded it further– weaving his own worldview into it. He wrote that if a man were to limit the scope of his work and instead specialized himself even further– he would save additional time and effort, easily beating a more general specialist with more experience. 

He discussed on the investment that both apprentices and craftsmen took by the virtue of their relationship– the first half of the apprentice's time dedicated to honing his craft, the second half repaying the master's time with his own. He wrote that this period of time could be shortened if a man were to limit the scope of his work and instead specialized himself even further. He essentially advocated for occupations to become even more narrow, to create a second degree of specialization. In an example quotes from Adam Smith, a less experienced specialist could beat a more general craftsman with more experience in terms of production– a ratio of 3:1. 

Babbage empathized the division of labour in his work, this entire chapter being "the causes of the advantage resulting from the division of labour." However, he believed that these three principles were incomplete– and so he devised a fourth one to perfect it. It is as follows: 

"that the master manufacturer, by dividing the work to be executed into different processes, each requiring different degrees of skill or of force, can purchase exactly that precise quantity of both which is necessary for each process; whereas, if the whole work were executed by one workman, that person must possess sufficient skill to perform the most difficult, and sufficient strength to execute the most laborious, of the operations into which the art is divided. "

He believed that the most optimal configuration of a workshop were a group of hyper-specialized craftsman paired with a supervisor to assign the division of labor and oversee the process. In this way, would machines of exact quality would be built at a greater pace– saving even more time for all involved parties.
