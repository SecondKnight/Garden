Good Morning TSGT Goode, Det 650. 

I am C/Warsame and today I will briefing you about Chief Master Sergeant Richard L. Etchberger, a medal of honor recipient. 

Richard Etchberger was the younger of two siblings born on March 5th, 1933 in Hamburg, Pennsylvania. His father, Donald Etchberger worked at a hosiery mill until Richard was eight– when Japan struck Pearl Harbor. After that, his father lost his job and then he and his brother Robert started to work odd jobs to support the family.

He was known to be a whiz with both academics and sports, distinguishing himself in school as part of the basketball team and school newspaper staff. Once he graduated from Hamburg High School in 1951, he enlisted into the Air Force a few weeks later. 

After he completed basic training, he took a course in electronics and in radar maintenance before qualifying as a radar operator. Originally, Etchberger wanted to be a pilot and successfully made into a Aviation Cadet program in 1954 before he was disqualified due to a previous head injury. 

It was his first time dealing with failure, so after his first assignment– he quit for 12 weeks before he reenlisted for another 12 years. In these years, he met his wife and became a father of three sons. He went through multiple reassignments and was eventually promoted to Chief Master Sargant in 1967– a year before the battle of Lima Site 85, his final assignment. 

At the time, the US was engaged in a Cold War against the Soviet Union and had signed an agreement preventing them from stationing troops in the Kingdom of Laos. To get around this, Etchberger was assigned to the 1043rd Radar Evaluation Squadron and placed for a CIA mission code-named 'Project Heavy Green' due to his expertise with radar systems. 

Him alongside a group of hand-picked Airmen were temporarily released from the Air Force to act as 'Civilians'. They were then hired by the Lockheed Corporation and were sent to a mountainous radar station known as 'Lima Site 85' and directed air strike missions in Laos and north Vietnam. 

Over their mission was over, they would rejoin the Air Force with their rank and benefits intact– ready for their next assignment. Over the course of 'Project Heavy Green', Lima Site 85 became responsible for 23% of all air strike missions in the region. Eventually, the Northern Vietnamese caught onto it and started to investigate. 

In the morning hours of March 11, 1968, the site was attacked by Vietnamese soldiers who scaled the surrounding cliffs. By 3am, Etchberger and seven others were the only American survivors out of 19 men. Given his profession as a Radar Technician, he never had any formal combat experience– but he still picked up arms and fought for the lives of his men. For hours, Etchberger single-handily held them off with an M-16 rifle and coordinated air rescues and strikes on top of his position.

Once the rescuers came, he risked his life several times to load the men onto the slings. But when he finally climbed onto one, he was hit by a burst of gunfire. He survived the initial helicopter flight but passed before he could receive further medical treatment. 

After the mission, his body returned to the US and was buried in his hometown. At the time, he received the Air Force Cross, the second highest military award for valor. This decision was made by Gen. John D. Ryan, former Air Force Chief of Staff. he agreed that Etchberger's actions warranted a medal of honor, it couldn't be awarded without national news action. 

In the end, it took multiple petitions, an Air Force personnel board review, and two acts of Congress for him to receive his medal. On September 21, 2010, his Air Force Cross was formally upgraded to a Medal of Honor and was presented to his sons at White House ceremony. 

In summary, Chief Master Sargant Richard L. Etchberger was a man who demonstrated a tenacious will and was courageous during the ordeals of Lima Site 85. He exemplified the phrase "Service before Self" when he gave his life for his fellow airmen. His conduct earned him the Purple Heart, Air Force Cross, and the Medal of Honor. He serves as a ideal for Airmen, be it past, present, and future.
