I am a feminist. Both thought and action align with this philosophy, but I don't like to call myself as one. After all, some of us believe in not equality– but supremacy. Some of us fight for women, but not for all women.  Others deride themselves into delusions, blaming not themselves for their woes– but this struggle. 

Feminism. At its core, it is an equalizer. One that uplifts women onto the same ground as men, ensuring that both enjoy the same probability. While outcomes remain undetermined, they share the same start. From this core rose four waves, each having their distinctive currents– three that I concur, one that I question. 

I recognize the first wave, one that fought for women in the battlefield of politics– advancing referendums relentlessly. Today, women vote thanks to their efforts, ones that saw them arrested, beaten, forcibly fed and restrained. It was them that waltzed into the Oval Office as a part of these efforts. It wasn't enough for them to fight back against men, but also their own folk– ladies stuck in the past, unable to move forward. 

For what its worth, in any effort that mankind perseveres towards– a counterweight will exist, however minor it is. Their reasoning may differ, but the outcome is the same as both predecessors and successors. Even now, we see their descendants prater on about a woman's place– contradictions that wish to snuff their compatriots' light. 

I pity their intelligence, one chained to such burdensome egos. But, spiting them isn't the point of this essay– at least not yet. I accept the first wave of feminism as part of my beliefs.

I recognize the second wave. A wave that cemented women's place in the workforce and in the economy. One that bore witness to Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, to Title VII of the Civil Rights Amendment of 1964, and Griswold v. Connecticut– a case that recognized the autonomy of Women. It was this era that took the gains of the first generation and left a perpetual boon as their inheritance– the right for their work to be treated as any other. 

There is no sound reason for me to object to any aspect of this. To do is to demean every second that a woman has worked, and to spit on the work of every man who fought on their behalf. If I am to do such, then I must give up the rights that my forefathers secured for me. Being a traitor is already a stain, no need to darken it with the mark of a hypocrite.


Now, I question the third wave beyond its continuation of the second wave. Unlike the first and second waves, there is no clear purpose that served as a banner.
